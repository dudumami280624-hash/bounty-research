THE MARKET MIND
LESSON 01

Your Stock Falls 10%. Why Does Your Plan Suddenly Change?

A stock falls 10%.

Yesterday, you said you would reassess if it dropped this far. Today, you say the market is overreacting. You decide to wait. Tomorrow, you may find another reason to wait.

Maybe holding is the right decision. Maybe selling is. The price decline alone cannot answer that question.

But something else happened before either decision: the standard you planned to use has changed now that the outcome feels personal.

That is where this course begins.

The Market Mind is about two things we should never confuse: what markets actually show us, and what our own minds add to the evidence. We will learn to read prices, trends, trading volume and risk. We will also learn how uncertainty, fear, excitement and hindsight can distort those readings.

Our first lesson isn't a stock pick or a chart pattern. It is a skill you can use before you put any money at risk: learning to separate a decision from the story you tell yourself afterward.

THE $100 DECISION

Consider a fictional stock, Northstar. The following example is hypothetical, not a record of any real company's trading.

You are watching Northstar at $100. You believe its recent trend may continue, but you are not certain. Before taking a position, you write down three things:

Your observation: the stock has been rising over the period you selected.

Your hypothesis: the move might continue if the conditions supporting it remain in place.

Your review condition: if the relevant evidence changes, you will reassess rather than automatically defend your original idea.

Then Northstar falls to $90.

What have you actually learned?

The price declined 10% from your reference point. That is a fact. Depending on the data, you might also know whether volume increased, whether a prior low was broken, and whether the broader market fell at the same time.

What don't you know?

You do not know from the price alone whether the company is permanently impaired, whether the decline is temporary, or whether the next move will be up or down.

This creates two separate questions.

First: what has changed in the evidence?

Second: how does that change affect the decision you said you would make?

If you answer only the first question with a story you invented after the decline, the second question disappears. You are no longer evaluating your plan. You are defending your position.

A PLAN IS NOT A PREDICTION

One of the source lessons behind this series makes a useful point: decisions made before a trade need an emergency plan. It asks students to consider adverse moves, when to reconsider an exit, what would justify a later re-entry, and how to protect a favorable outcome.

That core idea deserves to survive. The promises of certain profits surrounding it do not.

A plan does not make a market predictable. It makes your response more explicit when the market does something you did not expect.

Think of the difference between these statements.

"I know the stock will rebound."

"If new information changes my investment case, I will reassess the position."

The first makes a claim about the future. The second describes a decision process.

Neither statement guarantees a good outcome, but only the second makes room for uncertainty and correction.

There is an additional distinction for a global audience. A long-term investor deciding whether the business remains attractive is not answering exactly the same question as a short-term trader evaluating a price pattern. Different purposes can lead to different review conditions. We should not pretend there is one universal exit rule for everyone.

THE THREE LINES THAT KEEP AN IDEA HONEST

Before you commit to an investment idea, try writing three lines.

Line one: What do I know?

Write only observations or independently verifiable information. For example: "The closing price is 10% below the price I recorded last week." Do not add a motive unless you have evidence for it.

Line two: What do I think it means?

This is your hypothesis. For example: "The move may indicate weakening short-term demand." The word "may" matters. It marks the difference between a possibility and a proven cause.

Line three: What would make me reconsider?

Identify new evidence, a changed assumption, or a condition requiring a fresh review. For a long-term investment, that might concern the business. For a trading hypothesis, it might concern a defined price structure or risk limit. The condition has to fit the question you're actually trying to answer.

Now imagine you are wrong.

The purpose of the third line isn't to protect your ego from being wrong. It is to make being wrong a situation you have considered in advance.

Notice what this exercise does not require. You do not need to know tomorrow's price. You do not need to recognize an advanced chart pattern. You do not need to pretend that every move has one identifiable cause.

You need to make your reasoning visible enough that you can challenge it later.

A GOOD OUTCOME CAN COME FROM A BAD PROCESS

Here is a second hypothetical example.

Two people buy the same stock at $100. Both see it fall to $90. Both decide to keep holding. A month later the stock rises to $115.

The eventual price is identical for both people. Does that mean their decisions were equally thoughtful?

Not necessarily.

One person had written down a long-term investment thesis, checked whether its underlying evidence had changed, considered the position's risks, and made a deliberate decision to continue holding.

The other person had no review criteria. They simply could not tolerate admitting a loss, so they refused to reconsider.

Both ended with the same favorable price movement. Yet one decision had a documented rationale; the other depended on hope.

Now reverse the ending. Suppose the stock fell further instead. That would not automatically prove that the first person's process was irrational. A thoughtful decision can have a disappointing outcome under uncertainty. Equally, a lucky outcome does not prove that an unexamined decision was sound.

This distinction is essential if you want to learn from your own history.

If you judge every decision solely by whether the next price move rewarded you, you may reinforce bad habits and discard good reasoning.

A better review asks: What information was available at the time? What assumptions did I make? Which risks did I recognize? What would I change about the process, regardless of the final price?

WHY THE EXIT RULE IS HARDER THAN IT SOUNDS

The original teaching material also reveals an important tension. In one passage, an exit condition depends on the closing price. In another, the speaker says to act when a price level is touched.

These are not identical rules.

Suppose a fictional stock trades below a chosen level during the day but closes above it. A rule requiring confirmation at the close could produce a different decision from a rule that responds to an intraday move.

Neither rule can be declared universally correct without knowing the investment purpose, the definition of the condition, and the risks of waiting or acting.

There is another complication: a personal review rule is not the same thing as an order you place with a broker.

In the standard U.S. framework described by the Securities and Exchange Commission, a stop order becomes a market order when its trigger condition is met. The stop price does not guarantee the execution price. A stop-limit order places a price boundary on execution but might not fill at all. Broker and market rules can differ.

Why bring this up in the first lesson?

Because "I will sell at $90" can mean several different things. Do you mean you will review the position if the closing price is at or below $90? Do you mean you will submit an order when that condition occurs? Or do you mean a broker-held stop order is already in place?

If you cannot distinguish those instructions, your plan isn't yet clear enough to test.

We will cover the mechanics in a later lesson. Today, the important insight is that a plan needs precise language, not just a comforting number.

THE MIND FILLS IN WHAT THE CHART LEAVES OUT

Markets produce observations. People produce explanations.

A price goes up, and someone says institutions are accumulating. A price drops, and someone declares manipulation. A rebound follows a sale, and the seller says, "I knew I should have waited."

Some explanations may prove true. Others may not. The visible price path is not sufficient evidence for every motive people assign to it.

This is where market analysis and investor psychology meet.

We want stories because stories make uncertainty feel manageable. But a story becomes dangerous when we stop asking what evidence would challenge it.

The solution is not to stop interpreting markets. It is to label interpretations correctly.

Observation: Northstar moved from $100 to $90.

Hypothesis: short-term selling pressure may have increased.

Unknown: who will trade next, why they will trade, and what price will follow.

Decision process: compare new evidence with the conditions established for the original idea.

That is the operating discipline this series will return to, whether we are reading a candlestick, examining volume or reviewing a financial statement.

YOUR FIRST PRACTICAL TEST

You do not need a live trade for this exercise. In fact, begin without one.

Choose a stock chart or a fictional price series. Imagine you are considering an investment idea. Write four short answers:

1. What exactly do I observe? Include the time period and the data you are using.

2. What is my interpretation? Identify it explicitly as a hypothesis.

3. What evidence would weaken or overturn that interpretation?

4. If that evidence appeared, what would I review before deciding what to do?

For example:

Observation: The price fell from $100 to $90 during the period I selected.

Hypothesis: The earlier upward trend may be weakening.

Counterevidence to my original idea: A sustained failure to recover a previously defined price area, alongside other relevant information.

Next review: Recheck the time horizon, the reason for the original idea, and the applicable risk conditions; do not improvise an explanation simply because the price moved against me.

There is no universal correct decision to buy, sell or hold in this exercise. That is deliberate. We are learning to structure a decision, not pretending to know the future.

If you can tell the difference between a fact, an interpretation, a condition and an action, you have learned something more valuable than a stock tip.

WHAT THIS SERIES WILL BUILD

The Market Mind will follow one question from beginning to end:

How do we turn uncertain market information into decisions we can explain, question and improve?

First, we will learn to read what the market records: price, time, candlesticks, trends, volume and the limits of each measure.

Then we'll examine how interpretations form: why a breakout may look convincing, what would count as contrary evidence, and why a chart cannot reveal every participant's intention.

Next come the mechanics and constraints: orders, liquidity, risk, exit conditions and the difference between a planned price and an actual execution.

Finally, we'll bring the information and the mind together: loss aversion, confirmation seeking, retrospective storytelling, and the discipline of reviewing decisions without rewriting history.

The point is not to build perfect predictions. It is to make better-informed, more accountable judgments under uncertainty.
