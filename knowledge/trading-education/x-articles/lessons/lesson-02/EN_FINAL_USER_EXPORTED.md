On Friday, August 28, 2020, Apple shares closed at $499.23.

On Monday, August 31, they closed at $129.04.

If you compare those two prices without any context, the result looks like a 74% collapse.

Imagine opening your brokerage app and seeing a number like that.

Did something disastrous happen to the company over the weekend? Did investors lose almost three-quarters of their money?

No.

Apple had completed a four-for-one stock split.

A shareholder who previously owned one share now owned four. The price of each share changed because the number of shares changed.

And here is the surprising part: based on those two closing prices, an investor who held through the split actually saw the market value of that holding rise by approximately 3.39%, not fall by 74%.

The prices were real.

The alarming interpretation was wrong.

Welcome to Lesson 02 of The Market Mind.

In Lesson 01, we raised a question about what price charts actually show—and what a closing price can leave out.

Before comparing prices across days or weeks, however, we need to ask something even more basic: are the prices we're comparing measured on the same basis?

Apple's 2020 stock split shows why that question matters.

Before asking whether a stock is going up or down, we need to know something more fundamental:

**What exactly are we looking at?**

## 1. The difference between a number and what it measures

A stock chart is a visual representation of market information.

It can show historical prices, trading periods, volume, and calculations based on earlier observations.

But numbers do not explain themselves.

A price is always a price for something, measured in a particular unit, at a particular time.

If the unit changes and we ignore that change, the comparison can become meaningless.

Imagine someone tells you that a pizza used to cost $20, but now costs $5.

That sounds like an enormous discount.

Then you discover that the original price was for one whole pizza, while the new price is for one quarter.

The price per item fell by 75%.

The price of an equivalent amount of pizza did not change.

A stock split creates a related measurement problem.

The company has not necessarily become less valuable just because each share represents a smaller fraction of it.

For that reason, reading a stock chart begins with identifying the unit being measured.

## 2. What actually happened to Apple?

Apple announced its four-for-one stock split on July 30, 2020.

The relevant dates were straightforward: August 24 was the record date, August 28 was the distribution date, and split-adjusted trading began on August 31.

For every old share, shareholders received three additional shares, bringing the total to four.

Now consider an investor who held one Apple share at the August 28 closing price of $499.23.

Before the split:

**1 share × $499.23 = $499.23**

The mechanically equivalent price after a four-for-one split was:

**$499.23 ÷ 4 = $124.8075 per share**

Four shares at that price would still be worth $499.23.

But the market traded at a different price on August 31. Apple's actual closing price was $129.04.

After the split:

**4 shares × $129.04 = $516.16**

The change in the holding's market value was:

**($516.16 − $499.23) ÷ $499.23 ≈ 3.39%**

That 3.39% reflects Apple's actual closing-price movement from August 28 to August 31 on a consistent, split-adjusted share basis. The stock split itself did not create that gain.

These figures ignore trading costs, taxes, and investor-specific circumstances. They compare the value of an equivalent holding across the split, not the results of buying or selling at those prices.

Now look at the two misleadingly different percentage calculations.

Compare the original per-share quotes:

**$499.23 to $129.04 = approximately −74.15%**

Compare prices using a consistent post-split share basis:

**$124.8075 to $129.04 = approximately +3.39%**

The first calculation is mathematically valid as arithmetic.

But it is not a meaningful measure of an unchanged shareholder's investment return, because the shares on the two dates represent different fractions of the company.

This is one of the most important lessons in financial analysis:

**A correct calculation applied to the wrong comparison can produce a false conclusion.  **

Apple's 2020 four-for-one stock split: original per-share quotes suggest a 74.15% decline, while comparable split-adjusted closing prices show a 3.39% increase from August 28 to August 31. The split itself did not create that gain.

## 3. Why your historical stock chart may look different

After a stock split, chart providers often restate older prices to make the historical series comparable.

For a four-for-one split, pre-split prices are divided by four.

Therefore, a historical price of $499.23 can be displayed as $124.8075 on a split-adjusted chart.

Nothing about the original transaction has changed.

Only the way that historical price is expressed has changed.

One data source may show a dramatic discontinuity around a split while another displays a continuous price series.

The problem becomes more complicated when dividends enter the picture.

Some providers adjust historical prices for both splits and dividends. Others let readers choose between different conventions.

And a setting named “unadjusted” may not mean what a beginner expects.

StockCharts provides a useful example: even when a chart is labeled “unadjusted” for dividends or distributions, its historical prices are still adjusted for stock splits. The meaning of an adjustment setting depends on the data provider.

The practical consequence is simple.

Before interpreting a striking price move, check whether you are seeing:

- Prices as originally quoted at the time;
- Prices restated for stock splits;
- Prices adjusted for splits and dividends or distributions.

Those are not necessarily interchangeable series.

A historical price chart is not just a picture of the past.

It is also the result of decisions about how the past should be represented.

## 4. What a candlestick actually records

Once the data convention is clear, we can begin reading the chart itself.

One of the most common formats is the candlestick.

A standard candle summarizes four values for a particular period:

**Open:** the opening price.

**High:** the highest recorded price.

**Low:** the lowest recorded price.

**Close:** the closing price.

These four values are known as OHLC.

The candle's body extends between the open and close.

Its upper and lower shadows, often called wicks, mark the high and low when those prices lie outside the body.

The precise definition of an opening or closing price depends on the market and data source. Some exchanges establish official prices through opening or closing auctions.

The candle also depends on the chosen interval.

One candle might represent five minutes.

Another might represent a full trading session.

A third might represent an entire week.

The same market activity can look dramatically different depending on how time is grouped.

But there is a deeper limitation.

Even when all four OHLC prices are correct, a candle does not show the complete sequence of trades.

## 5. One candle, two possible trading days

Let's create a fictional stock called Northstar.

Its daily candle contains these prices:

Open: $100
High: $110
Low: $95
Close: $105

We can verify two things immediately.

The stock closed 5% above its open.

Its highest and lowest prices were $15 apart.

Now consider two possible intraday sequences.

**Path A:** $100 → $95 → $110 → $105

**Path B:** $100 → $110 → $95 → $105

Both paths produce the same daily open, high, low, and close. 

Two different intraday price paths can produce the same daily candlestick. Both have an open of $100, a high of $110, a low of $95, and a close of $105. The candle alone cannot reveal which price came first.

But they describe very different sequences of market activity.

In the first, the low comes before the high.

In the second, the high comes before the low.

The daily candle cannot tell you which sequence occurred.

It also cannot tell you who traded near those prices, what news participants were reacting to, or what they intended to do next.

A candle summarizes selected price observations.

It is not a recording of every market event.

That is why a long lower wick cannot, by itself, prove that an institution deliberately defended the stock.

The shape is observable.

The identity and motives of the traders are not established by the shape.

We can investigate those questions, but we should not pretend that the candle has already answered them.

## 6. The five-line chart receipt

The Market Mind is not a course about memorizing mysterious signals.

I want readers to develop a habit they can use whenever a stock chart seems to tell a convincing story.

Call it the **Five-Line Chart Receipt**.

Before accepting an explanation, write down five things.

**1. Security**

What instrument is shown, and where is it traded?

**2. Timeframe**

What period does each observation represent? Which trading session is included?

**3. Data basis**

Are these original historical quotes, split-adjusted prices, or prices adjusted for other corporate actions?

**4. Verified observation**

What fact can I establish from the numbers themselves?

**5. Unproven interpretation**

What explanation, attribution, or prediction has been added, and what further evidence would support it?

Now apply the method to Apple's 2020 split.

Security: Apple common stock, AAPL, listed on Nasdaq.

Timeframe: daily closing prices for August 28 and August 31, 2020.

Data basis: original per-share quotes compared with prices restated for the four-for-one split.

Verified observation: the originally quoted closing prices were $499.23 and $129.04.

Unproven or mistaken interpretation: shareholders suffered a roughly 74% loss simply because the per-share price changed.

The mistake becomes obvious once the share-count adjustment is included.

Now apply the same method to Northstar.

Verified observation: the fictional stock opened at $100 and closed at $105.

Unproven interpretation: a particular investor group caused the move or the stock must rise the next day.

Notice the difference.

The chart has not become less useful.

You have simply become more precise about what it establishes.

## 7. Try this before your next market decision

Open a historical chart for a company you know.

Do not place a trade.

First, find the ticker, exchange, timeframe, and data provider.

Next, check whether the price series is adjusted for splits or dividends.

Then select one trading day and record the open, high, low, and close.

Write two sentences.

The first should describe something you can verify from the data.

For example:

“The stock closed above its opening price.”

The second should describe something the chart alone cannot establish.

For example:

“Institutional investors were secretly accumulating shares.”

Now ask what additional evidence would be necessary to test that second statement.

You may need company disclosures, regulatory filings, trade-level data, or other reliable information.

Sometimes the necessary evidence will not be available.

That is an acceptable conclusion.

An unanswered question is better than a confident explanation without evidence.

## 8. What changes when you learn to read the evidence?

Apple's four-for-one split is a powerful example because the arithmetic is simple and the error is easy to make.

A price can be real.

A percentage calculation can be correct.

And the conclusion can still be wrong because the comparison uses inconsistent units.

A candlestick creates a related problem.

Its four prices can be accurate while leaving out the sequence of trading and the reasons behind it.

These examples lead to one principle:

**Before interpreting a market number, make sure you understand what it measures.**

Before assigning a cause, identify evidence for that cause.

Before making a decision, distinguish the observation from the story.

That is how we begin to think independently about markets.

Not by rejecting charts.

Not by treating every technical pattern as meaningless.

But by refusing to ask the data to prove more than it actually can.
