THE MARKET MIND

LESSON 01

In the first eleven months of the year, investors in one major study were more willing to sell their winning stocks than their losing ones.

Then December arrived.

The pattern reversed.

Suddenly, losses were more likely to be realized than gains.

Same brokerage accounts. Same definitions of profit and loss. A different decision environment.

What changed?

The familiar explanation is that people enjoy taking profits but hate admitting mistakes.

That might help explain part of the pattern. But it can't explain the entire story.

And that's the problem with many confident statements about financial markets: they sound convincing until you examine what the evidence actually says.

This is where The Market Mind begins.

Not with a stock recommendation or a promise about the next bull market.

With a more useful question:

How do we tell the difference between what the market shows us and what our minds persuade us to believe?

THE DECEMBER REVERSAL

In 1998, finance researcher Terrance Odean published a paper in The Journal of Finance titled "Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?"

He examined trading records from 10,000 accounts at a U.S. discount brokerage firm, covering 1987 through 1993.

The paper studied the disposition effect, a tendency to sell investments showing gains more readily than investments showing losses.

But Odean didn't simply count the number of winners and losers sold.

That distinction matters.

Imagine an investor owns eight stocks with unrealized gains and two with unrealized losses.

If that investor sells three winners and one loser, they've sold more winners. But they also had more winners available to sell.

A simple count wouldn't tell us enough.

So Odean compared the gains and losses investors actually realized with the opportunities they had to realize them.

The study's main results were striking.

Across the full year, the proportion of gains realized was 14.8%.

The proportion of losses realized was 9.8%.

From January through November, those figures were 15.2% and 9.4%.

In December, they changed to 10.8% and 12.8%.

> 图 2：12 月反转柱状图

Investors realized gains more frequently than losses from January to November, but the pattern reversed in December. These figures represent realization rates, not investment returns. Source: Odean (1998), Table I.

The relationship flipped.

These figures are realization rates. They are not investment returns, the percentage of investors who made money, or the probability that a trade will succeed.

They describe selling behavior under specific definitions.

Why did December look different?

One important factor was taxes.

Under relevant U.S. tax rules, realizing a loss before year-end can have consequences that make selling more attractive. The paper found evidence consistent with tax-motivated selling.

The study also examined competing explanations, including portfolio rebalancing and transaction costs. Those factors did not fully explain the broader tendency.

But we should be careful about what the findings mean.

This was one historical U.S. brokerage sample, not a universal experiment involving investors everywhere.

Tax rules and investor circumstances differ across countries.

And holding a losing stock is not automatically an irrational decision.

The research points to something more interesting than a simple emotional weakness.

An investor's response to the same kind of gain or loss can change when the circumstances surrounding the decision change.

That brings us to the first question every investor should learn to ask:

What changed?

Was it the investment itself?

Was it the evidence?

Or was it the meaning of that investment to the person making the decision?

THE NUMBER YOUR STOCK DOESN'T REMEMBER

Consider a fictional company called Northstar.

You buy its shares at $100.

A month later, they trade at $80.

You tell yourself:

"I'll sell when I get back to even."

It's a familiar thought.

But examine the logic.

Why should $100 determine what happens next?

Because that's what you paid.

Your purchase price matters for calculating your return. It may also affect taxes, transaction costs, and portfolio decisions.

But the company doesn't know what you paid.

Neither does the next buyer.

Your cost basis is not a forecast of future market value.

A new investor looking at Northstar today sees a stock priced at $80. They must examine its business, valuation, risks, and their own objectives.

Your $100 purchase price isn't automatically relevant to their assessment.

This is where a personal reference point can begin to masquerade as an investment argument.

There's nothing inherently wrong with continuing to hold a stock that has declined.

The business may remain attractive. The lower price may even improve its potential value under certain assumptions.

But "I want to get my money back" is a statement about your circumstances, not evidence that the stock should recover.

Try a question that removes your original purchase from the picture.

If you had never owned Northstar, would today's evidence make you interested in it at $80?

You might say yes.

You might say no.

You might need more information.

The question is not an instruction to buy or sell. Existing shareholders and prospective buyers can face different constraints.

It simply forces you to examine whether the past price is influencing your judgment more than the current evidence.

THREE THINGS THAT CAN CHANGE

Now imagine Northstar has fallen 20%.

Before buying, you believed three things:

The company's revenue would grow.

Its debt would remain manageable.

And the market price was reasonable under your assumptions.

A month later, the stock trades at $80.

There are three different kinds of change to investigate.

The first is the market price.

That is easy to observe.

The stock is down 20% from your purchase price.

The second is the investment evidence.

Has the company reported weaker results?

Have its financial obligations increased?

Have management's expectations changed?

Or has the broader market fallen while the company's outlook remains relatively stable?

The third is your own decision environment.

Do you need cash sooner than expected?

Has the position become too large relative to your portfolio?

Are tax considerations affecting your choices?

Or are you reluctant to sell because realizing a loss would feel like admitting you were wrong?

These questions are not interchangeable.

A price decline is an observation.

"The business is deteriorating" is an interpretation that requires evidence.

"The stock must recover" is a forecast that cannot be established from the decline alone.

If you change your conclusion because new evidence undermines your assumptions, that may be good reasoning.

If you change your explanation simply to avoid reconsidering a position, you may be protecting a story rather than testing an investment thesis.

The difference matters.

A plan isn't useful merely because it exists.

It must identify what you believed, what would challenge that belief, and what you would review if the evidence changed.

And even a clearly written plan can be wrong. Consistency doesn't turn a weak strategy into a strong one.

A REAL MARKET EVENT WITH AN INCOMPLETE STORY

The same reasoning problem appears when we try to explain dramatic market events.

GameStop in January 2021 is a useful example.

According to a subsequent U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission staff report, GameStop closed at $347.51 on January 27.

The next day, its shares reached an intraday high of $483.

> 图 3：GameStop 折线图

GameStop's January 2021 price surge. The $483 figure represents an intraday high, not a closing price. Source: SEC Staff Report (2021).

Trading volume was extraordinary.

Short interest was substantial.

Social-media attention was intense.

Some retail brokers temporarily restricted certain transactions.

A widely repeated explanation was that short sellers were being forced to buy back shares to close their positions, pushing the price higher.

Was that entirely wrong?

No.

The SEC staff report found that short covering likely contributed to the price increases during certain periods.

But it wasn't the whole explanation.

Buying by traders covering short positions represented only a small fraction of overall buying volume during the relevant period.

And GameStop's price remained elevated after the direct effects of short covering would have faded.

The report pointed to broader positive sentiment as an important factor in sustaining the rally.

That creates a crucial distinction.

"Short covering contributed to the rise" is a statement supported by part of the evidence.

"Short covering explains the entire rally" goes much further.

The second claim requires more support than the first.

The report also acknowledged limits in identifying the motivations behind market participants' activity.

We can observe trading.

We cannot automatically know what every buyer or seller believed.

And we should not confuse the two sources used in this lesson.

Odean's paper studied selling behavior across brokerage accounts.

The GameStop staff report examined an extraordinary trading event.

GameStop does not prove that its traders exhibited the disposition effect described in Odean's study.

The connection is a method of thinking.

In the first example, we questioned a behavioral explanation by examining how selling rates changed across months.

In the second, we questioned a market explanation by examining what the available trading evidence could actually establish.

Both show why the first plausible story shouldn't always be the final one.

READ THE CLAIM BACKWARD

Here's a habit we'll use throughout The Market Mind.

When you encounter a confident market statement, don't begin by asking whether it sounds persuasive.

Start by asking what evidence would have to exist for the statement to be reliable.

We will examine claims through four questions.

MARKET
What happened that can be independently verified?

MIND
What explanation, expectation, or personal reference point am I adding?

EVIDENCE
What supports that explanation? What challenges it? What's still unknown?

DECISION
What conclusion, if any, is justified by the information available and the question I'm trying to answer?

This is an editorial teaching framework, not a trading strategy or a newly discovered financial theory.

Let's use it on two examples.

CLAIM ONE
"Investors never want to sell stocks at a loss."

What can we verify?
Odean found a greater tendency to realize gains than losses across most of the year in his sample.

What is being added?
The word "never."
It turns a documented tendency into an absolute rule.

What challenges it?
The December reversal.

What conclusion follows?
Investors' selling behavior can vary with incentives and circumstances. The absolute claim is unsupported.

CLAIM TWO
"High short interest guarantees a GameStop-style rally."

What can we verify?
GameStop had substantial short interest and experienced an extraordinary increase in its share price.

What is being added?
The assumption that one factor explains the entire event and predicts similar outcomes elsewhere.

What challenges it?
The SEC staff report documented significant buying beyond short covering, along with broader market dynamics.

What conclusion follows?
One historical example does not establish a universal trading rule.

Notice what happened in both cases.

We didn't reject the underlying observations.

We rejected conclusions that claimed more than those observations could prove.

That's a skill worth developing before learning advanced indicators or trading patterns.

WHY A PROFITABLE DECISION CAN STILL BE POORLY REASONED

Consider two hypothetical investors.

Both buy Northstar at $100.

Both watch it fall to $80.

Both decide to hold.

Eventually, the stock rises to $120.

The outcome is the same.

But one investor reviewed the company's prospects, checked new information, evaluated the risks, and deliberately maintained the position.

The other refused to examine anything that challenged the original decision.

They simply believed the stock had to recover.

Both investors benefited when it rose.

That doesn't mean their reasoning was equally sound.

Now imagine the stock fell to $60 instead.

That wouldn't automatically mean the first investor's process was irrational.

A thoughtful decision can have a poor outcome.

A poorly reasoned decision can occasionally produce a profit.

Results matter, particularly when judging an investment approach over a suitable number of observations.

But a single outcome is not a complete assessment of one decision.

To learn from a decision, we need to know what was believed before the outcome became known.

That's why recording your reasoning matters.

Otherwise, hindsight can turn uncertainty into an illusion of foresight.

YOUR FIRST MARKET MIND EXERCISE

Choose one financial claim you've encountered recently.

It might concern an individual stock, an index, a company's earnings, or a market event.

Don't place a trade for this exercise.

Write the claim down as precisely as possible.

Then answer four questions.

What can I verify?

What interpretation is being added?

What evidence would challenge that interpretation?

What conclusion does the available information justify?

If you're using a chart, record the security and time period.

If you're relying on a research statistic, check what the number measures.

If you're using a historical example, distinguish the documented event from the story told about it.

Now add one final question:

What would make me change my mind?

A valid answer cannot simply be "nothing."

Some evidence should be capable of weakening your interpretation.

If the answer is that you don't know enough yet, write that down.

Uncertainty is not a failure of analysis.

Pretending uncertainty has disappeared is the bigger problem.

Save your answers with the date.

Return to them when new information arrives.

Did the evidence change?

Did your interpretation change?

Or did your personal circumstances change?

Those are different things, and they deserve different responses.

WHAT THIS FIRST LESSON REALLY TEACHES

The market produces prices, transactions, financial reports, and events.

Our minds turn them into explanations.

Sometimes those explanations are well supported.

Sometimes they are shaped by our purchase price, tax incentives, fear of regret, or a persuasive story that leaves out important facts.

The December reversal in Odean's research shows why investor behavior cannot always be reduced to one emotional slogan.

The GameStop report shows why an extraordinary market event cannot always be explained by a single familiar cause.

Neither tells us what will happen tomorrow.

Together, they teach a more durable habit.

Before trusting a conclusion, examine the evidence that supports it.

Before defending your own conclusion, ask what could prove it incomplete or wrong.

That is where The Market Mind starts.

NEXT LESSON
What Does a Stock Chart Actually Record?

Today, we questioned the relationship between market observations and the explanations attached to them.

Next, we'll examine the observations themselves.

What does a closing price represent?

Why can the same stock appear strong on a weekly chart and weak on a daily chart?

And what gets lost when we look only at a line connecting closing prices?

Before interpreting a chart pattern, we need to know what the chart actually contains.

## Lesson 02

- 标题：Apple's Stock Went From $499 to $129. Why Wasn't That a 74% Crash?

On Friday, August 28, 2020, Apple shares closed at $499.23.

On Monday, August 31, they closed at $129.04.

If you compare those two prices without any context, the result looks like a 74% collapse.

Imagine opening your brokerage app and seeing a number like that.

Did something disastrous happen to the company over the weekend? Did investors lose almost three-quarters of their money?

No.

Apple had completed a four-for-one stock split.

A shareholder who previously owned one share now owned four. The price of each share changed because the number of shares changed.

And here is the surprising part: based on those two closing prices, an investor who held through the split actually saw the market value of that holding rise by approximately 3.39%, not fall by 74%.

The prices were real.

The alarming interpretation was wrong.

Welcome to Lesson 02 of The Market Mind.

In Lesson 01, we raised a question about what price charts actually show—and what a closing price can leave out.

Before comparing prices across days or weeks, however, we need to ask something even more basic: are the prices we're comparing measured on the same basis?

Apple's 2020 stock split shows why that question matters.

Before asking whether a stock is going up or down, we need to know something more fundamental:

What exactly are we looking at?

1. The difference between a number and what it measures

A stock chart is a visual representation of market information.

It can show historical prices, trading periods, volume, and calculations based on earlier observations.

But numbers do not explain themselves.

A price is always a price for something, measured in a particular unit, at a particular time.

If the unit changes and we ignore that change, the comparison can become meaningless.

Imagine someone tells you that a pizza used to cost $20, but now costs $5.

That sounds like an enormous discount.

Then you discover that the original price was for one whole pizza, while the new price is for one quarter.

The price per item fell by 75%.

The price of an equivalent amount of pizza did not change.

A stock split creates a related measurement problem.

The company has not necessarily become less valuable just because each share represents a smaller fraction of it.

For that reason, reading a stock chart begins with identifying the unit being measured.

2. What actually happened to Apple?

Apple announced its four-for-one stock split on July 30, 2020.

The relevant dates were straightforward: August 24 was the record date, August 28 was the distribution date, and split-adjusted trading began on August 31.

For every old share, shareholders received three additional shares, bringing the total to four.

Now consider an investor who held one Apple share at the August 28 closing price of $499.23.

Before the split:
1 share × $499.23 = $499.23

The mechanically equivalent price after a four-for-one split was:
$499.23 ÷ 4 = $124.8075 per share

Four shares at that price would still be worth $499.23.

But the market traded at a different price on August 31. Apple's actual closing price was $129.04.

After the split:
4 shares × $129.04 = $516.16

The change in the holding's market value was:
($516.16 − $499.23) ÷ $499.23 ≈ 3.39%

That 3.39% reflects Apple's actual closing-price movement from August 28 to August 31 on a consistent, split-adjusted share basis. The stock split itself did not create that gain.

These figures ignore trading costs, taxes, and investor-specific circumstances. They compare the value of an equivalent holding across the split, not the results of buying or selling at those prices.

Now look at the two misleadingly different percentage calculations.

Compare the original per-share quotes:
$499.23 to $129.04 = approximately −74.15%

Compare prices using a consistent post-split share basis:
$124.8075 to $129.04 = approximately +3.39%

The first calculation is mathematically valid as arithmetic.

But it is not a meaningful measure of an unchanged shareholder's investment return, because the shares on the two dates represent different fractions of the company.

This is one of the most important lessons in financial analysis:

A correct calculation applied to the wrong comparison can produce a false conclusion.

> 图 2：苹果拆股左右对比图

Apple's 2020 four-for-one stock split: original per-share quotes suggest a 74.15% decline, while comparable split-adjusted closing prices show a 3.39% increase from August 28 to August 31. The split itself did not create that gain.

3. Why your historical stock chart may look different

After a stock split, chart providers often restate older prices to make the historical series comparable.

For a four-for-one split, pre-split prices are divided by four.

Therefore, a historical price of $499.23 can be displayed as $124.8075 on a split-adjusted chart.

Nothing about the original transaction has changed.

Only the way that historical price is expressed has changed.

One data source may show a dramatic discontinuity around a split while another displays a continuous price series.

The problem becomes more complicated when dividends enter the picture.

Some providers adjust historical prices for both splits and dividends. Others let readers choose between different conventions.

And a setting named "unadjusted" may not mean what a beginner expects.

StockCharts provides a useful example: even when a chart is labeled "unadjusted" for dividends or distributions, its historical prices are still adjusted for stock splits. The meaning of an adjustment setting depends on the data provider.

The practical consequence is simple.

Before interpreting a striking price move, check whether you are seeing:

Prices as originally quoted at the time;

Prices restated for stock splits;

Prices adjusted for splits and dividends or distributions.

Those are not necessarily interchangeable series.

A historical price chart is not just a picture of the past.

It is also the result of decisions about how the past should be represented.

4. What a candlestick actually records

Once the data convention is clear, we can begin reading the chart itself.

One of the most common formats is the candlestick.

A standard candle summarizes four values for a particular period:

Open: the opening price.
High: the highest recorded price.
Low: the lowest recorded price.
Close: the closing price.

These four values are known as OHLC.

The candle's body extends between the open and close.

Its upper and lower shadows, often called wicks, mark the high and low when those prices lie outside the body.

The precise definition of an opening or closing price depends on the market and data source. Some exchanges establish official prices through opening or closing auctions.

The candle also depends on the chosen interval.

One candle might represent five minutes.

Another might represent a full trading session.

A third might represent an entire week.

The same market activity can look dramatically different depending on how time is grouped.

But there is a deeper limitation.

Even when all four OHLC prices are correct, a candle does not show the complete sequence of trades.

5. One candle, two possible trading days

Let's create a fictional stock called Northstar.

Its daily candle contains these prices:
Open: $100
High: $110
Low: $95
Close: $105

We can verify two things immediately.

The stock closed 5% above its open.

Its highest and lowest prices were $15 apart.

Now consider two possible intraday sequences.

Path A: $100 → $95 → $110 → $105
Path B: $100 → $110 → $95 → $105

Both paths produce the same daily open, high, low, and close.

> 图 3：一根 K 线两条路径图

Two different intraday price paths can produce the same daily candlestick. Both have an open of $100, a high of $110, a low of $95, and a close of $105. The candle alone cannot reveal which price came first.

But they describe very different sequences of market activity.

In the first, the low comes before the high.

In the second, the high comes before the low.

The daily candle cannot tell you which sequence occurred.

It also cannot tell you who traded near those prices, what news participants were reacting to, or what they intended to do next.

A candle summarizes selected price observations.

It is not a recording of every market event.

That is why a long lower wick cannot, by itself, prove that an institution deliberately defended the stock.

The shape is observable.

The identity and motives of the traders are not established by the shape.

We can investigate those questions, but we should not pretend that the candle has already answered them.

6. The five-line chart receipt

The Market Mind is not a course about memorizing mysterious signals.

I want readers to develop a habit they can use whenever a stock chart seems to tell a convincing story.

Call it the Five-Line Chart Receipt.

Before accepting an explanation, write down five things.

1. Security
What instrument is shown, and where is it traded?

2. Timeframe
What period does each observation represent? Which trading session is included?

3. Data basis
Are these original historical quotes, split-adjusted prices, or prices adjusted for other corporate actions?

4. Verified observation
What fact can I establish from the numbers themselves?

5. Unproven interpretation
What explanation, attribution, or prediction has been added, and what further evidence would support it?

Now apply the method to Apple's 2020 split.

Security: Apple common stock, AAPL, listed on Nasdaq.
Timeframe: daily closing prices for August 28 and August 31, 2020.
Data basis: original per-share quotes compared with prices restated for the four-for-one split.
Verified observation: the originally quoted closing prices were $499.23 and $129.04.
Unproven or mistaken interpretation: shareholders suffered a roughly 74% loss simply because the per-share price changed.

The mistake becomes obvious once the share-count adjustment is included.

Now apply the same method to Northstar.

Verified observation: the fictional stock opened at $100 and closed at $105.
Unproven interpretation: a particular investor group caused the move or the stock must rise the next day.

Notice the difference.

The chart has not become less useful.

You have simply become more precise about what it establishes.

7. Try this before your next market decision

Open a historical chart for a company you know.

Do not place a trade.

First, find the ticker, exchange, timeframe, and data provider.

Next, check whether the price series is adjusted for splits or dividends.

Then select one trading day and record the open, high, low, and close.

Write two sentences.

The first should describe something you can verify from the data.
For example:
"The stock closed above its opening price."

The second should describe something the chart alone cannot establish.
For example:
"Institutional investors were secretly accumulating shares."

Now ask what additional evidence would be necessary to test that second statement.

You may need company disclosures, regulatory filings, trade-level data, or other reliable information.

Sometimes the necessary evidence will not be available.

That is an acceptable conclusion.

An unanswered question is better than a confident explanation without evidence.

8. What changes when you learn to read the evidence?

Apple's four-for-one split is a powerful example because the arithmetic is simple and the error is easy to make.

A price can be real.

A percentage calculation can be correct.

And the conclusion can still be wrong because the comparison uses inconsistent units.

A candlestick creates a related problem.

Its four prices can be accurate while leaving out the sequence of trading and the reasons behind it.

These examples lead to one principle:

Before interpreting a market number, make sure you understand what it measures.

Before assigning a cause, identify evidence for that cause.

Before making a decision, distinguish the observation from the story.

That is how we begin to think independently about markets.

Not by rejecting charts.

Not by treating every technical pattern as meaningless.

But by refusing to ask the data to prove more than it actually can.
